🎨 HCI & UI Design

Human-Computer Interaction principles and user-centred interface design – Unit 4.

1. What is HCI?

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the study of how people interact with computers and how to design systems that are easy, efficient, and enjoyable to use.

Why HCI Matters

  • Poor UI design leads to user errors, frustration, and abandoned software.
  • Good design reduces cognitive load and improves productivity.
  • Accessibility ensures everyone – including people with disabilities – can use technology.

2. Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics

These ten principles, developed by Jakob Nielsen, are a standard checklist for good UI design.

  1. Visibility of system status – keep users informed about what is happening (e.g. loading bars).
  2. Match between system and real world – use familiar language and concepts.
  3. User control and freedom – provide undo/redo to reverse mistakes.
  4. Consistency and standards – follow platform conventions.
  5. Error prevention – design to prevent errors before they occur.
  6. Recognition over recall – make options visible rather than relying on memory.
  7. Flexibility and efficiency – support both novice and expert users (e.g. keyboard shortcuts).
  8. Aesthetic and minimalist design – avoid irrelevant information.
  9. Help users recognise, diagnose, and recover from errors – plain-language error messages.
  10. Help and documentation – provide easy-to-search support.

3. UI Design Elements

Layout & Visual Hierarchy

  • Use whitespace (negative space) to avoid clutter.
  • Larger, bolder elements draw attention first.
  • Align elements consistently to create order.
  • Use the F-pattern or Z-pattern for how users scan a page.

Colour

  • Choose a limited palette (primary, secondary, accent).
  • Ensure sufficient contrast between text and background (WCAG AA requires a ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text).
  • Never use colour as the only way to convey information (colour-blind accessibility).

Typography

  • Use readable fonts – sans-serif (e.g. Arial, Open Sans) for body text.
  • Maintain hierarchy: headings > subheadings > body text.
  • Minimum body font size: 16 px for screen readability.

Navigation

  • Users should always know where they are and how to get back.
  • Breadcrumbs, menus, and clear labelling aid orientation.
  • Limit menu items to 5–9 (Miller's Law / cognitive load).

4. User-Centred Design (UCD)

UCD places the user at every stage of the design process.

Design Process Stages

  1. Research – understand users, their needs, and context (surveys, interviews, personas).
  2. Define – identify the problem and requirements (functional & non-functional).
  3. Ideate – brainstorm solutions; create low-fidelity sketches.
  4. Prototype – build a mockup (wireframe, Figma prototype, HTML prototype).
  5. Test – evaluate usability with real users; gather feedback.
  6. Iterate – refine based on feedback and repeat.

Prototyping Types

  • Low-fidelity (lo-fi) – hand-drawn sketches or simple wireframes. Fast and cheap.
  • High-fidelity (hi-fi) – detailed, interactive mock-ups closely resembling the final product.

5. Accessibility (a11y)

Accessible design ensures digital solutions can be used by people with a wide range of abilities.

WCAG Principles (POUR)

  • Perceivable – information must be presented in ways users can perceive (alt text for images).
  • Operable – UI components must be operable (keyboard navigation).
  • Understandable – information and UI operation must be understandable.
  • Robust – content must be reliably interpreted by assistive technologies.
